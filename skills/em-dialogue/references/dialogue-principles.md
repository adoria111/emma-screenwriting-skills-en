# Two-Step Dialogue Refinement: Principles and Calibration

Source method version: 2026-11-11. Emma's dialogue practice rendered as executable rules. The short excerpts below are fictional calibration examples, not project material; English examples are explanatory adaptations.

## Principles

1. Establish a compelling scene first: distinctive character, effective image, concrete desire/opposition. Director/promotional perspectives identify core anticipation and images, not compulsory production expansion.
2. Let characters pursue motives through everyday speech. Use specific objects, resources, costs, and demands rather than abstract judgments. “Speak from the heart” does not mean reciting psychological self-analysis.
3. Make essential relationships, desire, and attitude understandable on one hearing. Reduce allusion and convoluted phrasing. This describes viewing conditions, not intelligence inferred from nationality/education.
4. Serve character appeal: bravery, vulnerability, wit, or boundaries. Give villains concrete pressure and children age-appropriate desires/expression. Do not share one aphoristic voice across the cast.
5. Before cutting, check plot, character, relationship, emotion, and performance losses. An unchanged external plot does not make a line useless. Explain less when existing action communicates accurately; subjective misunderstanding/private judgment may still need inner voice or VO.
6. Ask in order: what would deletion lose, can it be more concise, is the character's demand clear? Preserve the emotional steps that make refusal, betrayal, protection, or resistance credible.

## Step handoff

Step 1 establishes goal, obstacle, effective image, character effect, knowledge boundary, emotional change, and facts. Step 2 judges contribution in that context, not isolated toughness. Identify event/action-design causes; dialogue-only permission still allows completion of feasible dialogue work.

F and E use 0–10 as editorial notes; S=max(F,E) interprets the legacy method rather than creating a scientific scale. Explain contextual function. Numbers, exclamation marks, and brevity do not earn points by themselves. Delete S<5; rewrite 5≤S<7. Correct a mistaken low score before deleting unique information or meaningful emotion.

## Fictional boundary cases

### A. Specific humiliation and responsibility

The scene establishes a stained $2,000 dress and a mother/child attending a matchmaking event. Villain: “You stained my dress! It's expensive! Two thousand dollars! Can you afford it? What are you poor people doing here?”

Possible compression: “This dress cost two thousand dollars. Can you afford it? What are you poor people doing here?” Retain amount, contempt, and exclusion; remove visible staining and repeated expense. Invent no brand, bespoke construction, or compensation terms.

If the mother's original response accepts responsibility and protects her child, retain that. Do not invent sudden wealth or denial for catharsis. Accountability and protection themselves create appeal.

### B. A child's charm may stand independently

A child holds bread too tough to bite: “It's bullying me. Mommy, eat it for me.” If context supports the child's perspective, do not delete solely because external events do not advance. It expresses a worldview, help-seeking tactic, and intimacy. Still check for repeated versions of the same joke.

### C. Viewer knowledge is not a confession

The audience saw a sister hide an acceptance letter. She tells the other sister the outside world is dangerous and she is protecting her. Preserve the mismatch between apparent care and control. An explicit confession of hiding the letter would reveal the secret and change later events.

### D. Concision does not fix a procedural scene

A clerk's lengthy instruction to fill a form, sit down, and wait for a supervisor can become “Fill this in, then wait over there for the supervisor.” If nothing else happens, the scene remains procedural. Dialogue-only permission does not allow adding humiliation, queue-jumping, or an identity reversal.
