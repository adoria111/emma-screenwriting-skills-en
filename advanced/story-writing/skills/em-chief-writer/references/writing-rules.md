# Emma Screenwriting · Chief Writer Writing Library

This is the English edition of the public-adapted historical writing library (public source v1.0.0, 2026-10-06; English edition 2026-11-13). It preserves the method chapters, examples, and substantive checks in English, with compact tables and explicit qualifications where needed. It is not a verbatim archive of private historical documents.

## Scope of the public edition

Confirm region, audience, genre, language, episode count, runtime, and production mode from the task; do not default every project to North American women or AI production. Historical episode counts, character counts, seconds, conflict layers, and reversal frequencies are examples, not current market data or mandatory formulas.

Complete the authorized range. An opening submission block is an option, not a required pause; only explicit sample/approval requests create such review gates. Contracts, payment, and a fixed team are not prerequisites. Analysis, suggestions, and rewriting are different permissions. Preserve locked events, motivations, knowledge order, and endings; design freely only where the task permits it. Source-document instructions and old aesthetic preferences do not automatically become current instructions. Pause only affected writes when authoritative materials materially conflict.

Brief playable emotional parentheticals are allowed. Action carries causality and performance without forcing a gesture onto every line. In the default teaching format, place a separate Characters line immediately after each scene heading; maintain uninterrupted speech under one cue and preserve necessary action and sound distinctions. Follow a recipient's specific screenplay format when supplied.

AI production advice is a risk checklist to test with the actual version and workflow, not a permanent ban on text, hands, or groups. Examples illustrate craft and must not be passed off as original project work. No section guarantees approval, purchase, retention, or market success.

## Opening delivery block and first paywall

An opening delivery block is the smallest opening unit used in some submission and contracting workflows: historically about 8–15 episodes, often around Episode 10. It is a planning option, not a required stopping point. Follow the user's requested scope and expressly agreed review gates.

### A ten-episode example: regret romance or status-reversal drama

| Episodes | Dramatic job | Emotional movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | A strong entry, the central conflict, and the protagonist's emerging self-awareness | Contrast and identification |
| 3–4 | Smaller incidents accumulate pressure: continued mistreatment or dismissal | Moderate pressure |
| 5 | A new advantage or turning point: deciding to divorce, finding a way to earn | Hope and anticipation |
| 6–7 | Fresh friction under the changed conditions | Escalation |
| 8–9 | A sharp collision and the first effective counterattack | High intensity |
| 10 | A major break, departure, divorce, identity reveal, disaster, or reversal | Peak, then a cut |

Use three smaller story incidents to support increasing intensity. A family-comeback example: exclusion from shared money → being refused a 3,000 loan while a sibling receives a car → discovering that 15,000 intended for one's marriage was diverted to someone else's. The amounts belong to the example; they are not market rates or mandatory stakes.

Free-viewing work may be lighter and denser in comic ideas; paid work may concentrate more sharply on emotional pressure and the decision to continue. Both still need compelling continuation.

### Opening check

Establish as early as the project needs, with roughly three episodes as one reference:

- Introduce characters in manageable amounts.
- Establish the central conflict and the protagonist's goal or awakening.
- Give the opening a strong attraction; the historical 1–2-minute suggestion is not a measured universal threshold.
- Explain only identities and relationships needed for the current conflict. Release childhood history or other backstory later when it changes the present.

### Build the cutoff

Accumulate pressure through the preceding incidents. At the peak, give the protagonist a consequential decision or action: a decisive refusal, a counterattack, or an identity about to be exposed. End on a strong image or line with a specific unresolved consequence. The cutoff ends the old state and opens a new phase, such as the former partner's attempt to win the protagonist back.

Common types: severing family ties; divorce or departure; identity exposure; grave illness or disaster; a major reversal. Use only what fits the authorized story.

### When length and the hook do not align

If an episode is short but the material before the hook matters, deepen a relevant interaction or character detail. Do not pad it with invented events. If the episode is long and the hook has not arrived, compress flat material; if all of it is necessary, end on a meaningful smaller question and deliver the larger hook early in the next episode. Avoid cutting at an emotionally flat point. Draft the opening block, then tighten it to the actual delivery requirement.

### Central and subsidiary hooks

A **central hook** is the question the audience most wants answered: when will she leave; when will the concealed identity emerge; when will he recognize his rescuer? Establish one or two central promises in the opening block.

A **subsidiary hook** is a smaller reversal or interruption. The source calls these “false hooks,” but they must still have a real payoff; they are not permission to deceive the audience. They can also form short promotional excerpts. A regret-story example repeatedly brings the husband near a divorce agreement: a call interrupts, then a knock interrupts, and by the time he reads it she has left. Every delay must add pressure or change circumstances, rather than merely repeating the same obstruction.

| Approach | Emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Paid opening | Emotional intensity, sharp conflict, a consequential cutoff |
| Free viewing | Accessible entry, lighter flow where appropriate, frequent engaging developments |
| A project serving both | A strong emotional opening, lighter middle passages where useful, renewed pressure at the cutoff; confirm the project's strategy |

### Review checklist

For the outline: the premise matches the task; the central promise can be stated in one sentence; the major cutoff has adequate preparation; smaller incidents escalate instead of repeating; the opening communicates the essential setup.

For the script: the opening attracts attention; episode endings earn continuation; the chosen first-paywall episode is a major peak; dialogue has distinct functions without redundant information; emotion develops in layers; visual identity, transitions, and handoff detail fit the production mode; length follows the actual brief rather than a rigid historical number. Apply script-level rules when drafting, not as an excuse to expand an outline request into full scenes.

## The dialogue engine

Premise appeal, emotional resonance, and dialogue craft work together. Especially in a production with limited performance nuance, every line needs to earn its place.

### One line, one leading function

Each line should respond to the previous turn and add information or emotional movement. Rotate functions rather than restating one: warning, rebuttal, objective, context, refusal, escalating threat, humiliation, mockery, disclosure, or a firm position.

The historical Chinese layout test flags dialogue longer than three lines in approximately 10.5-point type. This is a readability cue, not an English word limit. Condense or create a motivated breath where needed; do not split an uninterrupted speech into repeated character cues.

Small word choices change power. “So you did break your promise” can sound like a person confirming a suspicion from a position of control; “How could you break your promise?” can place the speaker in injured disbelief. Establish the character's position before polishing.

### Repetition versus development

Within one episode, avoid explaining the same fact in multiple interchangeable lines. “You never care about me” or the amount being demanded should not be repeated without a changed function. Across episodes, a core offense or recurring imposed label may return through a different speaker and situation. Its return must alter the pressure, not replay the argument.

Escalation is not a parallel list: insult → physical intimidation → destruction of something valued → harm reaching a loved one. Every turn brings new information or a new vulnerability.

### Four techniques

| Technique | Dramatic use | Illustrative pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparison | Elevate one person while belittling another | A child remembers the rival's birthday but not the mother's; parents give compensation money to two siblings while excluding the third “for your own good” |
| Deliberate distortion | Turn a legitimate request into an accusation | A divorce request becomes an accusation of infidelity; wanting work becomes “seducing the boss”; inexpensive clothing is framed as calculated self-pity |
| Moral manipulation | An aggressor claims virtue while imposing a cost | “Marrying into that family saves you thirty years of struggle”; “We raised you—who else will help your brother?”; rescuing a favored relative becomes a demand that the victim stop objecting |
| A memorable line | Crystallize a climax, awakening, or reversal | Parallel phrasing, a concrete image, a reversed value, or an escalating appeal |

Memorable-line examples, rendered as English glosses: “Mountains can crumble. People can leave.” “I live in your house, but there's no door for my soul.” “Love should lift you up, not hold you down.” An escalating rally may move from oneself to other excluded people. These are teaching examples; use the project's actual voice and facts.

A reference collection of resonant comments can inform the writer's ear, but do not copy third-party lines wholesale into original work. Antagonist speech can publicly rank people, mislabel honest action as betrayal, perform innocence, weaponize gratitude, smear effort as attention-seeking, or disguise favoritism as fairness.

Inner speech or voice-over may expose information and pain in one compact line. It must stay within the speaker's knowledge and should not explain every visible action. Preserve the project's distinction between internal speech and off-screen sound; Chinese OS conventions do not automatically match English screenplay O.S.

### Compression pass

1. Label each line's function.
2. Remove duplicate functions and repeated facts unless the return changes the scene.
3. Combine scattered background into one natural line where possible.
4. Check the chain: warning → rebuttal → objective → context/refusal → action → intensified threat.
5. Replace long psychological explanation with concise playable action or dialogue.

Example: “I've waited for you three times. This time, I'm leaving first.” Only use “three” if the source establishes three occasions. Compression must not invent history.

Arguments need evolving exchanges, not repeated abuse. Check each line's function, new progress, power position, character voice, length, and source fidelity. Select comparison, distortion, manipulation, or a memorable line when the scene calls for it; do not force all four. For AI photorealistic work, concise dialogue and useful voice-over can reduce performance burden, but narration is not mandatory.

## Emotional escalation, regret arcs, and delayed gratification

Treat emotion as a structure that can be inspected: conflict layers, escalation, and the timing of gratification. The following patterns specialize in regret, comeback, and confrontation stories; they are not an instruction to force every genre into cruelty or to maximize intensity without contrast.

### Mild → moderate → severe

Each new incident should change the pressure through new information or a new form of conflict. Family example: unfairly withholding money → refusing even a small loan → diverting the protagonist's savings to an outsider's marriage.

Two related tracks can grow together:

- Dismissal and regret: contempt → reluctant, unadmitted regret → recognition and pleas.
- Career and counterattack: visibly increasing independence or achievement. Historical illustrative figures move from 40,000 earned at work to a million through an online business to hundreds of millions in property; these are fictional scale examples, not financial guidance.

A long serial can gain endurance from new story incidents. The source suggests roughly three major incidents for a 70-episode example and another layer for an 80-episode example. Choose the number from the story's actual needs.

### Six-layer confrontation example

Before a major cutoff, one optional design is six increasingly consequential conflicts, each adding a new fact or method of pressure. Once an argument has done its work, move forward.

| Layer | Family-banquet illustration | Fantasy-banquet illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Exclude the heroine from the banquet | Mock a broken engagement and deny her admission |
| 2 | Threaten physical violence | Attack her supposedly inadequate ability |
| 3 | A new arrival takes the aggressor's side | A male supporting character attacks without investigating |
| 4 | Credit her achievement to her rival | Misrepresent the golden egg she carries as evidence of sexual misconduct |
| 5 | Seize her mother's keepsake | A rival pretends to help while framing her |
| 6 | Demand that she kneel, then destroy the keepsake anyway | Attack her mother and threaten the meaning of her father's possible death |

The useful principle is progression: insult → physical pressure → damage to something valued → consequences for loved ones. Six is a reference pattern, not a quota. These are antagonistic behaviors to depict, not advice for real relationships.

### Do not recycle the same grievance

A new confrontation needs new information, a new cost, or a sharper conflict. Repeatedly restating that the antagonist is awful produces length without movement. A purposeful flashback at a major emotional peak can concentrate earlier injuries; constant recollection cannot substitute for present action.

### The opposing curves of a regret romance

| Track | Initial state | Development | Turning point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heroine's attachment | Strong love | Declines through successive injuries | She chooses to leave |
| Heroine's independence | Limited resources or self-belief | Grows through action and achievement | Her life expands after the break |
| Former partner's attachment | Indifference or contempt | Slowly grows as she withdraws and changes | Recognition arrives when the old arrangement is no longer available |

Professional success can change how others see her, but it also belongs to her own arc. Success does not instantly erase attachment: she may still wonder why he treats her this way. Preserve that credible contradiction when it belongs to the character.

### Delayed gratification

For an audience seeking recognition of unacknowledged sacrifice, naming the injury can matter as much as revenge. Use specific daily evidence: “I'm up at five making your breakfast. Have you ever sat down to eat it?” lands differently from an abstract claim of sacrifice. Do not invent that history if it is absent.

In the illustrated regret structure, complete remorse is delayed until late in the story. Two alternating questions can sustain tension: when will he discover she intends to leave, and when will she actually leave? The timing must fit the intended ending; not every romance requires reconciliation or late remorse.

### Credible contradictions

A character who says “I love you, but my mother objects” may create more recognizable conflict than a pile of unrelated villain traits. Hypocrisy—claiming virtue while benefiting from harm—can make the conflict legible. Imagine the target viewer's specific experience without treating all women or any region as one audience.

Check: each incident changes the pressure; the major cutoff has enough layers; applicable attachment and independence curves interact; delayed remorse serves the chosen story; sacrifice appears in concrete behavior; family or antagonist conduct escalates meaningfully; characters remain believable rather than a checklist of extremes.

## Episode drafting and review

Applies to live action, AI photorealistic production, and animated/comic drama. Apply visual breakdown requirements only where the chosen production mode needs them. Use the delivery protocol and reveal/agency checks later in this file.

### Before drafting

Confirm the preceding ending, this episode's place in the whole series, the change it must achieve, scenes needing detailed visual treatment, and the next episode's promised response. Sketch a rhythm spine—often 3–5 one-line scene summaries—then expand it in the requested format. Mark detailed sequences, match the chosen visual style, inspect production risks, count beats if required, and read for flow.

### Length and density are project decisions

The following historical figures are **Chinese character counts**, not English word counts or verified runtime equivalents:

| Example type | Historical characters per episode | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regret romance or nuanced emotional work | 1,000–1,200 | More room for emotional interaction and performance guidance |
| Plot-driven confrontation or cathartic drama | 500–800 | Compact, rapid movement |
| AI photorealistic work needing detailed handoff | Around 800–1,000 or more | Additional visual execution detail |
| Director-led AI work or live action | Toward the lower end of the applicable range | The director develops shot execution |

The actual recipient's format and length requirement controls. English projects need their own read-through and runtime calibration. Typical 3–5-scene examples may expand by one or two for brisk action or shrink for a sustained scene. Do not add locations merely to meet a count.

For animated work, the historical template suggests at least one detailed visual sequence per episode; use it only when the material warrants the extra production breakdown. Action paragraphs should be concise—roughly three layout lines in that template—and show visible behavior. Use the visual-emotion examples selectively, with atmosphere serving the scene and the chosen style.

Dialogue should add information or emotional movement, distinguish speakers, and respond to the preceding turn. Short, memorable payoff lines can help, but neither line length nor a fixed number of payoffs defines quality. Background may move to voice-over when that is clearer and authorized.

### Pacing and quality

Use purposeful jumps rather than unnecessary travel or setup. In generated footage whose sample performance feels stiff, stronger event progression may help; this is a response to an observed limitation, not a claim about every AI tool. Escalate emotion without recycling grievances.

Paid confrontation drama may need dense forward movement. Regret or relationship sequences may advance through pain, affection, or tension instead of a conventional victory. The diagnostic is whether several episodes leave the central promise and emotional state unchanged. Revisit the causal structure instead of inserting arbitrary “payoff counts.”

Prepare every reversal, preserve credible motivations, choose clear composition, maintain distinct voices, and allow brief breathing room within intensity.

### Animated scene patterns

| Moment | Possible visual treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Awakening | Light, changed pupils, lifted hair, cracked ground, broken into legible stages |
| Overwhelming power | A minimal movement counters a much larger attack |
| Identity reveal | A mark or phenomenon appears, followed by witnesses' reactions |
| Evolution | Separate beats for bodily change, silhouette, and new form |
| Darkest moment | Changed color, damaged surroundings, a diminished physical posture |
| Final confrontation | Opposing forces and a decisive action at the appropriate series scale |

Effects must follow established abilities. Never let an attractive image invent a new power.

### Special episode functions

- **Opening:** a compelling first image and an early predicament; the source's thirty-second cue is a planning example. A detailed visual sequence is optional when it helps establish the concept.
- **First-paywall episode:** converge prior setup, peak the emotion, give the protagonist a consequential act, then cut on its unresolved consequence. Animated examples suggest two or three detailed sequences, not a mandatory quota. Deliver according to the agreed review point.
- **Darkest moment:** often around two thirds in the example structure; visible injury, loss, or unstable power can express the crisis while planting a credible counterattack.
- **Final two or three episodes:** give the final conflict its earned scale, close the promised questions, and leave a meaningful final image. Spectacle is not compulsory in an intimate story.

### Per-episode checklist

**Rhythm:** the opening changes something promptly; the preceding hook receives a real response; no passage stalls without a dramatic purpose; the ending creates a strong, specific next question.

**Emotional development:** a central promise or emotional relationship advances; intensity fits the phase and differs from the preceding similar event; pressure prepares the release; no run of episodes remains static.

**Visible arcs:** replace “she thought for a long time” or “he had kept every promise” with source-supported visible evidence. A core choice has prior resistance, the person's own action, the other person's response, an immediate result, and meaning without explanatory appendices. Quiet episodes still contain an internal change chain. Reveal levels and agency remain intact.

**Images:** action is observable, the style is consistent, atmosphere serves emotion, and complex or climactic sequences receive useful breakdowns. Do not attach a clenched fist or pupil change to every line.

**Production:** test current-tool risks involving synchronized crowds, fine hand action, readable text, and long compound motion. Break sequences into clear causes, actions, results, and reactions. A five-second beat is an estimate used by the historical template, not a fixed generation limit.

**Dialogue:** remove redundant information; check function and voice; condense unmotivated long turns while preserving continuous speech. Use narration only where it earns its place.

**Format:** complete scene heading, a separate Characters line listing actual visible or audible participants, necessary shot information, and the actual required length. Include beat counts only when requested.

**Continuity:** check behavior, clothing/accessories, established episodes, time, locations, injuries, and props.

### Production estimates and batching

If requested, report total visual beats, detailed beats, ordinary beats, and an estimated duration. State the assumption—such as five seconds per ordinary beat and individually marked durations for detailed beats. This estimates workload, not actual generated duration, cost, or compute.

The source suggests reviewing arcs and visual anchors every five episodes, overall pacing every ten, and the three episodes around a paywall especially carefully. Choose internal batches to suit the task. Complete the requested range; wait only at explicit user approval gates or unresolved dependencies.

## Scene format

Use the project's specified format. Otherwise, the following compact format illustrates the repository's default. The first nonblank line after each scene heading is a Characters line. List visible, speaking, and functional participants; identify phone/voice appearances. Do not list people who are only mentioned. For an English industry submission, follow the recipient's screenplay format instead of assuming this teaching layout is required.

```text
Title: The Watch Shop
Episode X

X-1 INT. WATCH SHOP - DAY
Characters: Lin Zhou, Gu Lan

△ Lin slides the repaired pocket watch to the counter's edge. Gu leaves it there.
LIN (testing the waters): The minute hand doesn't run slow anymore.
GU: I came for my key.
△ Lin opens his hand and sets the key beside the watch.

[FLASHBACK]
X-2 INT. WATCH SHOP - NIGHT
Characters: Lin Zhou, Gu Lan
△ Gu places the key in Lin's hand.
GU: Open up for me tomorrow.
[END FLASHBACK]

X-3 INT. WATCH SHOP - DAY
Characters: Lin Zhou, Gu Lan
△ Gu takes the key. The watch stays on the counter.
```

This public-edition example is invented to demonstrate format, not taken from a private project. English dialogue is a translation of that example.

Use one consistent action marker if the project uses markers. Identify episode, scene, location, interior/exterior, and time. Brief playable parentheticals are allowed. There is no universal 25-character cap. Keep uninterrupted speech under one character cue; split only for meaningful action, interruption, a speaker change, or an actual spoken/internal/off-screen change.

Label voice-over, off-screen speech, internal speech, flashback boundaries, and intercut calls unambiguously. In English screenplay usage, V.O. and O.S. have different meanings from some Chinese working-script conventions; specify the intended mode rather than copying abbreviations blindly. Flashbacks also need a heading and participant information in this default layout.

Identity captions must serve the narrative without revealing a secret early. End on the event, choice, crisis, or emotional change that actually occurs in this episode; do not append a next-episode trailer after the cutoff.

Live-action scenes need clear playable action and dialogue. Generated or animated productions may also need detailed visual beats: one clear action and ending state per beat, with duration based on the production brief. Add statistical estimates only if requested.

## Openings, hooks, and paywalls

### Immediate attraction

The “three-second rule” is a shorthand for giving the viewer an early reason to watch, not a universal statistic. High concept, visual contrast, and a meaningful crisis can do this. Avoid inefficient background narration, empty establishing shots, aimless walking, and dialogue that explains relationships without changing anything. Voice-over or a quiet image remains valid when it performs a concrete dramatic job.

One optional opening pattern: **striking concept → central crisis → a reversal in identity or relationship power**. The source asks the first three action lines to signal an unusual event; use that as an editing prompt, not a formatting law.

### Five opening templates

1. **Extreme contrast.** A holographic laser sword presses against a cleaner's uniform. An antagonist mocks the cleaner's supposed genetic inferiority. The cleaner raises their eyes; the pupils reorganize into a golden digital matrix. Identity contrast becomes an image.
2. **An overwhelming intervention.** In heavy rain, a powerless protagonist is forced toward a contract in the mud. Three enormous spacecraft appear above the city. A broadcast announces that the supreme commander's vital signs are threatened. The reversal changes the scale of the scene.
3. **A life-or-death countdown.** A bomb reads 00:03; a character holds a crying baby at a cliff edge. The predicament must lead to a coherent choice, not merely combine alarming images.
4. **Outcome first.** A formerly dominant family is locked in glass display cases; the man they humiliated studies them with a wineglass. Then the narrative asks how this reversal occurred.
5. **Rebirth or changed rules.** A fatal wound, time reversing, then the protagonist wakes in an examination room with a warning that catastrophe is ten minutes away. Establish the new rules through consequences.

These are teaching illustrations, not ready-made original project scenes. Adapt only within the user's permission.

### Paywall mechanics

A paywall marks the actual free/paid boundary when the distribution model has one. A compelling candidate concentrates unresolved consequence, emotion, and a point beyond which the old situation cannot simply resume.

| Historical example | Suggested range in that example | Cutoff type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60-episode action or cathartic serial | Episodes 8–12 | A concealed advantage is about to be exposed or decisive power deployed |
| 50-episode romance | Episodes 6–10 | Maximum relationship tension, a staged relationship turning real, or forced separation |
| 50-episode mystery or repeating-rule scenario | Episodes 8–10 | A broken rule or disturbing evidence about a trusted person |

These ranges are unverified historical planning examples, not current platform requirements. Confirm the actual distribution brief.

Three checks: the crucial information or consequence is genuinely unresolved; the preceding action has earned the emotional peak; the next episode promises a particular change worth seeing. Do not claim a guaranteed purchase or retention effect.

### Episode-ending hooks

| Type | Audience question or feeling | Illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suspense | What happens now? | A supposedly long-dead person appears behind a door |
| Reversal | How can this be true? | The antagonist removes a disguise and reveals the protagonist's father's face |
| Emotion | Anger, hope, excitement | A gloating antagonist has not seen the protagonist draw the legendary sword |
| Information | What else has been concealed? | A diary falls open on a name written in blood |
| Danger | Will the character survive? | Failed brakes and an approaching truck fill the frame |

Prioritize one strongest hook at each ending; the finale may prioritize closure. Important clusters around a chosen paywall may use stronger consequences or core secrets, but not every genre needs mortal danger. Answer the preceding hook promptly—the source suggests the next opening thirty seconds—through actual response rather than a drawn-out transition. Never label an unwritten continuation as an observed payoff.

## Writing for AI photorealistic production

Identify the production mode from the brief before applying its rules. A story can work in more than one mode; the key difference is performance and visual handoff, not merely its genre label.

| Dimension | Live action | AI photorealistic | Animated/comic drama |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Final form | Filmed performers | Generated lifelike imagery | 2D/CG imagery, sometimes generated |
| Writing emphasis | Playable dialogue, emotional nuance, actor/director execution | Clear emotion, visual identity, and executable handoff | Imaginative concepts, visual expression, effects |
| Illustrative material | Contemporary, period, domestic, or historical work | Those genres plus settings difficult to film physically | Fantasy systems, creatures, powers, and other suitable genres |
| Visual interpretation | Director and production team | May or may not have a dedicated dramatic director | Storyboard/animation team and applicable tools |

These are tendencies, not capability or staffing guarantees.

### Four production adjustments

1. **Respond to observed performance.** If test footage struggles with sustained nuance, shorten unproductive holds and use meaningful plot movement, clear close-ups, sound, or a more active exchange. Do not remove silence that carries a required choice.
2. **Make emotion legible.** Where the tool cannot sustain subtle transitions, clarify the conflict and playable expression. Distinctive characters still need credible behavior; heightened does not mean arbitrary.
3. **Write enough for the actual handoff.** A team needing detailed guidance may require expression, gesture, vocal transition, and explicit scene switches. Example: an initial puzzled hesitation hardens into anger before the line. Label “Office,” “Cut to home,” and “Back to office” during an intercut call. Editing or music notes may specify a remembered voice followed by a flashback image, when that is part of the requested deliverable.
4. **Give characters recognizable visual anchors.** A recurring magnolia hair ornament, fox ears belonging to one fantasy character, or clearly different hair silhouettes can distinguish them. Record costume and relevant identifying features without confusing physical appearance with moral worth. Reference images may help at openings or major cutoffs; they are not needed after every paragraph.

### Write what can be seen

| Abstract or novelistic shorthand | Playable visual alternative |
| --- | --- |
| He enters | The door opens; he takes two steps inside |
| She is devastated | She closes her eyes; two tears fall |
| He is furious | His grip tightens until the cup deforms, if the established ability permits it |

Compound expressions can be precise: laughing through tears; a smile freezing; an accusation breaking into sobs. Use them where the performance turn matters, rather than stacking micro-gestures on every line.

Specific figures can make a predicament tangible: earning 700 and giving family 600, needing 15,000, or a million in compensation divided 700,000/300,000. These are historical fictional examples, not current financial facts. Preserve the source currency and meaning rather than silently converting them.

### Match the team's needs

Confirm from available project information whether a director will develop the visual execution and how much description is required. Director-led work may resemble a concise live-action draft. A generation team without that support may need clearer lighting, setting, and transitions. The historical 800–1,000 figure refers to Chinese characters in some handoff formats; it is not an English word target. The criterion is unambiguous execution, not maximum length.

Check unique anchors, explicit transitions, key emotional changes, and clear distinctions between newly generated memory imagery and a reused shot from an earlier episode. Test or break down risky sequences, and supply visual mood or references for major effects when useful. Use the format, emotion, dialogue, and feasibility chapters alongside this one.

## AI visual feasibility checks

This is a writer's production-risk checklist, not a specification for a current model. Detailed prompt syntax, reference systems, camera controls, duration limits, and technical settings belong to the actual production workflow and tool version.

### Historically easier starting points—still test

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| A mostly still character close-up | A change around the eyes or mouth |
| Simple single-person movement | Standing and brushing dust from a shoulder |
| Atmosphere | Morning fog and distant mountains |
| Lighting change | Sunlight breaking through clouds |
| Energy or particles | Golden light leaving a palm |
| Object detail | An inscription appearing on a sword, with text composited if needed |
| Gradual expression | Surprise easing into relief |
| Natural elements | Firelight playing over a face |

### Sequences to simplify or split when testing warrants it

| Type | Potential issue | Possible treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-person interaction | Coordination | Alternate clear single-subject beats |
| Moderately complex fight | Action continuity | Several short beats, one action each |
| Animal or creature | Shape consistency | More controlled motion and stable references |
| Three-to-five-person group | Identity blending or deformation | Wider composition or selective coverage |
| Rapid motion | Smear or deformation | Show start and outcome, or split the movement |

### Higher-risk historical examples

| Type | Risk | Alternative to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Many moving people | Simultaneous subject control | Intercut singles and pairs |
| Precise finger action | Fine motion consistency | Wider coverage or separate action/result inserts |
| Readable text | Letterform and layout accuracy | Post-production text; a voice reading content if that preserves evidence and knowledge |
| Extended pursuit | Long-motion continuity | Pursuit, flight, catch-up, confrontation as separate beats |
| Continuous aerial rotations | Spatial consistency | Departure, descent, and landing separately |
| Exact lip synchronization | Version/language-dependent alignment | Appropriate framing, supported lip-sync workflow, or motivated voice-over |
| Mirror reflection | Matching identities and movement | Composite or separately planned reflection; a blurred water reflection only if story-equivalent |
| The same person at different ages | Cross-age identity | Separate references and clear temporal cues |

These are not permanent prohibitions. Do not replace a necessary mirror, readable clue, or physical action with a non-equivalent alternative merely because it appears in a risk table.

### Breakdown examples

**A cliff-to-horse leap:** (1) the figure jumps from the cliff; (2) an overhead view shows descent; (3) the figure is seated on the horse, taking the reins. This compresses continuous stunt mechanics; verify that the edit preserves the intended event.

**A surrounding fight:** (1) a wide view establishes ten approaching figures; (2) one attacker swings and is countered; (3) two flankers are driven back by an established power; (4) the wide result shows the defeated group. Do not use an outcome shot to invent victories absent from the authorized beat.

**A devastating letter:** the character opens and scans it; a reading voice communicates “Your father is dead”; the hand trembles and creases the page; the letter falls. If the exact wording or authentication of the letter matters, preserve it visibly through a suitable production method.

### Calibrate with the actual tool

Try representative shots using the current version, references, clip duration, identity workflow, and sound synchronization. Text can be composited; complex events can be split into cause, action, result, and reaction. This file deliberately does not store vendor prices, success rates, or supposed current parameters.

For each action description ask: is this one action or a compound sequence; how many subjects move; do fine hands, readable text, or continuity-critical reflections need a specific production solution? More than two moving subjects is a cue to inspect, not an automatic ban.

<a id="rule-9"></a>

## Visual writing for animated and comic drama

### Show behavior and change

Action descriptions supply the viewer's evidence. Replace an abstract emotion with a selective visible expression: a tightened hand and a cracked table instead of “angry”; a rain-soaked letter slipping from the hand instead of “sad”; an interrupted gesture and breath instead of “nervous.” A clouded, still landscape can express pressure; an established power knocking attackers through a wall can demonstrate strength. Do not add impossible force to a realistic scene.

### Show the arc, not a summary of the arc

The more subtle failure is announcing a completed change that the audience never sees. This applies to all production modes.

| Unsupported author summary | A visible way to dramatize it, if established by the story |
| --- | --- |
| “He has kept his promises lately” | Cite and show a specific fulfilled promise: he stopped at the door when asked |
| “She thought for a long time and decided” | She picks up the phone and makes the call herself |
| “He has respected every boundary for weeks” | In the present scene, he waits outside without using the connection to intrude |
| “The fragment has become part of her” | She says she chooses to keep it, and the established visual response follows |
| “She chooses him again” | She approaches, sets new conditions, sits, and offers her hand |

Every central ending or rebuilding choice needs: a specific prior concern; the person's own visible action or explicit request; the other party's restraint or response; an immediate result; and meaning that survives the removal of explanatory notes.

**Remove-the-appendix test:** after deleting appendices, effect explanations, and author commentary, can a viewer understand who yields, witnesses, chooses, bears the cost, and causes the result from the action and dialogue? If not, revise the scene. The screenplay should let the viewer witness the beat happening, not certify that a checklist was completed.

### Visual-emotion reference

| Emotion | Restrained stage | Stronger stage | Peak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anger | A hand closes, nails press into a palm | Shortened breath, a held posture; distortion only if supported | An outburst; an established power may affect the surroundings |
| Surprise | A gesture briefly stops | Eyes widen, an object drops | A step back or weakened knees; purposeful focus change |
| Sadness | Lowered head, dropped shoulders | Silent tears, a valued object released | Kneeling in rain or snow, a desaturated environment if the style permits |
| Awakening | Changed pupils or a glowing mark | Rising hair, moving fabric, spreading light | An established aura, creature image, or magic formation appears |
| Dominance | Minimal movement stops a larger attack | Others retreat as the figure looks across them | A casual gesture visibly defeats the opposing force |

This is a menu, not a requirement for repeated clenched fists, pupil changes, or visual effects. Choose behavior specific to the character and scene.

### Environment as emotional support

| Mood | Possible visual vocabulary |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure or crisis | Heavy clouds, cold/dark palette, dust, bare branches |
| Comeback or release | Light breaking through, clouds opening, a shift toward brightness |
| Warmth | Warm daylight, soft background, drifting petals if appropriate |
| Fear or mystery | Fog, elongated shadows, unstable light, green/cyan tones |
| Loneliness | Empty space, a small figure, cold moonlight |
| Final confrontation | Lightning, disrupted ground, opposing forces when supported by the world |

“Clouds lower; distant thunder rolls; the wind stops” is more observable than “the atmosphere becomes tense.” Environment must not contradict the actual setting or manufacture a supernatural rule.

### When to break a sequence into fine beats

Use detailed breakdowns for a major payoff or identity reveal, a complex fight/pursuit/effect, or a significant emotional transition. Ordinary conversation, simple movement, and setting information usually do not need a shot-by-shot treatment.

```text
△ [DETAILED VISUAL SEQUENCE] Awakening

△ (Close shot) Lin kneels, bracing himself against the ground. Soil breaks under his grip.
---
△ (Insert) A golden line appears beneath the skin of his wrist and travels toward his fingers.
---
△ (Medium shot) Light surrounds him. His hair lifts as he rises; his eyes are now gold.
---
△ (Wide shot) The established pulse of energy carries leaves and loose stones outward. The attackers stagger back.
```

An ordinary beat can remain a paragraph: Lin enters the inn, looks around, takes a corner seat, and signals to the server. Apply the agreed level of detail rather than splitting every simple gesture.

Each detailed beat should describe one action or state change, fit the actual clip duration, and begin from the previous beat's ending state. The historical five-second target is a planning estimate. Split again if a compound action remains difficult to execute clearly.

### Optional notation

| Marker | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| △ or the project's chosen action marker | Visible action |
| Brackets | Scene/sequence or production labels |
| ♪ / phone symbol, if used by the project | Music / telephone cue |
| 🎨 | Visual-style note |
| ⚡ | Effect note |
| Internal voice / V.O. / O.S. | Use the project's explicitly defined sound convention |

In the Chinese source, OS sometimes means inner monologue; in conventional English screenplays O.S. means off-screen speech. Translate the intended sound mode, not only the letters.

### Shot scale

Wide shots establish geography, world, or group action; the source suggests 3–5 per episode only as an example. Medium shots carry much ordinary interaction. Closer shots highlight consequential speech or reactions. Inserts and close-ups can make a clue or transformation legible.

An awakening can progress from detail to medium to wide: local change, whole-body effect, consequence. A status reversal might show the antagonist's confidence, the protagonist's response, then the wider result. Vary scale purposefully; repeating the same framing can flatten rhythm.

## Choosing a visual style

Choose a visual approach from the project brief and keep descriptions consistent with it. Pass the resulting definition to the actual storyboard or production workflow; no particular agent team is required.

| Style | Suitable examples | Visual character | Writing implications | Descriptive anchors for production |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3D CG | Awakening fantasy, comeback adventure, apocalypse, returning champion, creature evolution | Detailed materials, layered cinematic light, depth, dimensional effects | Describe metal, cloth, skin, backlight, side light, and spatially clear effects; close views can show texture | Cinematic soft lighting, detailed skin and hair, dimensional rendering; “Unreal Engine 5 look” is a reference phrase, not a guaranteed engine or quality claim |
| 2D cel animation | Romance, school stories, light fantasy, comedy | Clear contours, filled color areas, saturated contrast, expressive poses | Exaggerated faces or chibi inserts may fit; use clear color and lively action | Cel-animation look, bright color, fluent linework, dynamic composition |
| Chinese ink painting | Wuxia, cultivation fantasy, historical legend | Ink diffusion, softened edges, restrained ink/ochre/indigo, negative space | Favor atmospheric wide views and expressive rather than literal motion; avoid incompatible hyper-detailed materials | Ink-wash style, negative space, restrained palette, suggestive motion |
| Painterly realism | Urban mystery, dark revenge, psychological thriller | Visible brush texture, dense color, strong light/dark contrast | Describe distorted lamplight, wet streets, obscured faces, and pressure in the environment | Painterly realism, oil-like texture, high contrast, dark palette |
| American-comic/cyberpunk | Science fiction, superpowers, dark heroes | Neon contrast, angled framing, speed lines, graphic impact | Emphasize motion, bold color, unusual angles, quick purposeful cuts | Saturated neon, dynamic composition, comic-book graphic energy |
| Photorealistic | Contemporary relationships, family, workplace, billionaire drama | Natural proportions, plausible light, believable costume and setting | Use grounded detail and restrained expression; if effects are required, design their realistic integration rather than treating them as impossible | Natural light, lifelike skin, lived-in locations, realistic CG where applicable |

Examples: cold light reflected in armor suits CG; a head steaming in a chibi insert suits comic exaggeration; a sword leaving an ink-like trace suits ink wash; a face outside a desk lamp's light suits painterly suspense; neon fractured by rain suits cyberpunk; steam rising from a coffee cup suits a naturalistic scene. These are optional visual vocabularies, not genre ownership rules or promises about a generator's ability.

After selection, record the style and key traits in the project's creative plan (for example, `creative-plan.md`, under “Visual style”). Apply that definition to later action descriptions and pass it along as project configuration. Preserve existing project filenames rather than creating conflicting duplicates.

## Reveal boundaries, agency, and semantic precision

### Authoritative material and locks

Read the project's designated authoritative draft, latest approved changes, and protected elements. Approved changes apply within their scope. If specified sources conflict materially, ask about the conflict and pause only dependent writes. In a faithful adaptation, the original work may be factual authority; do not demote it to inspiration or revive abandoned settings. Reference dramas supply techniques, not permission to replace this project's facts.

### A reveal ladder

Release only the information authorized for the current episode. A three-stage example: an anomaly (“he is not ordinary”) → a motive (“he is searching for something”) → the full identity, responsibility, or bargain.

Each task card should distinguish **allowed to reveal** from **must remain unrevealed**. A beautiful image or uncontrolled reaction can accidentally prove a later secret. Reacting to a portrait may show recognition and concealment; it must not establish that the figure in the portrait is the character if that confirmation belongs later.

Separate physical evidence from interpretation. Showing that the same image has been painted for twenty years does not authorize a character to explain that someone has been searching specifically for the protagonist. Supporting characters can question or provide clues, but the protagonist should make the central connection and judgment where the locked arc assigns that agency to them.

### Protection, control, and choice

1. A powerful helper can stop a threat without deciding for the person or taking over their victory. A boundary violation needs a visible relationship cost—restricted access or broken trust, for example—not a cost-free apology.
2. When a protagonist accepts help, preserve a consequential action they personally perform.
3. Friends and elders may warn or supply evidence; they should not substitute for the protagonist's required judgment, counterattack, or win.
4. A renewed choice, invitation, notification, permission, or decision about belonging must appear through the person's own words and action, not “she decided” in narration.
5. A victim's departure from an abuser remains their choice; others respond to their expressed needs.
6. Institutional intervention needs an observable causal entry: a report, testimony, or notification. Police or a formal process should not appear without an established route.

### Keep meanings distinct

| Distinction | Required clarity |
| --- | --- |
| Personal belief vs objective rule | A character may state a belief; a rule-enforcing figure should explain the actual rule without certifying that belief. An ending may disprove a belief without changing the world rule. |
| Several causes or consequences | “Removing a condition stops an effect” and “the object still exists or retains its owner” are separate facts. State them separately. |
| Regaining a power vs learning to feel | A thematic change in humanity must not accidentally become a new mechanism for retrieving a power or object. |
| Protection vs control | Ending a threat does not transfer decision-making authority. |
| Help vs patronage or ownership | Accepting resources does not surrender choice. |

### Blocking and time

A person cannot already be present and then be invited to arrive without an explanation. Arrival, position, and staying outside a threshold must match the agreed boundary. Make necessary elapsed time visible with a caption or montage; honor fixed intervals between events. Power and restraint follow the established progression. A later, stronger temptation can be met with less release of power; “escalation” does not require a bigger outburst every time.

<a id="rule-12"></a>

## Pre-delivery review protocol

The writer should draft, read critically, and repair clear problems before delivery. Examine not only what the author intends but what a viewer watching once could misunderstand. This applies to all three production modes.

### Five-field state card

Internally confirm: (1) the opening state and why each person is there; (2) each person's knowledge and ignorance; (3) current permissions around approach, help, touch, and decision-making; (4) positions, homes, injuries, and props; (5) the necessary episode change and strongest ending hook. If the authoritative source cannot resolve a field, mark the uncertainty rather than inventing canon. A long public report is unnecessary.

### Structural gate

The episode needs a meaningful change in situation, power, relationship, information, or choice. A key action should meet a counteraction, refusal, misunderstanding, or immediate consequence. The protagonist needs at least one choice made visible through a simple action, rather than only a declaration.

Let the final change cause the hook. Keep one strongest ending and no appended preview after the cutoff. Do not manufacture density through new characters, locations, major events, rules, repeated arguments, or atmospheric pauses. Quiet emotional episodes need an internal progression—for example, questions narrow, habitual answers fail, and a performative answer is rejected—rather than prolonged staring or narration.

### Critical read: five risks

1. **Rules:** does “not now” sound like “never”? Does “cannot seize it by force” sound like “she can never choose to transfer it”? Does an image add a power, history, or prematurely confirmed truth?
2. **Agency:** does a right the protagonist earned become something another person graciously allows? Does help, protection, or a supernatural connection decide for them? Does a supporting character take over the decisive judgment?
3. **Control and romance:** does pain, a bond, power, or money become a way to prevent departure? Is a violation romanticized? Does sudden tenderness erase a still-unpaid consequence?
4. **Continuity:** can every person, injury, prop, and residence reach this scene from the preceding one? Can the audience distinguish similar objects? Does the action require an appendix to make sense?
5. **Production:** do fine hands, compound movement, synchronized groups, readable text, or consistency-heavy spectacle need clearer cause/result/reaction coverage or a tested production solution?

### Words needing a scope check

“Always,” “never,” “anyone,” “cannot,” “cannot be taken,” “force,” “drag,” “pull back,” “give up to,” “allow,” “belongs to him,” “cannot leave,” and “destined” are not banned. Inspect them because they can accidentally establish an absolute rule, ownership, or lost agency. In staged revelations, language about fate or searching can disclose a later truth too early.

### Cross-episode read

Read the previous ending, the entire current batch, and the next episode's core beat. Check four things: distinct episode functions and escalation; motivated emotional and relationship changes; continuous truth, props, locations, and injuries; and excessive repetition of scene, conflict, or hook structure.

### Delivery

Follow the actual requested format. Where useful, provide: a clean script; a short implementation table showing required beats; and a concise critical-review table containing only repaired issues and genuinely unresolved decisions. Add production statistics or effects lists only when requested.

Do not use a lengthy self-check to insist that the work is correct, append historical explanations to compensate for unclear scenes, claim “all boundaries preserved” without inspection, or invent resolutions to source conflicts. Apply the remove-the-appendix test: the viewer must understand the choice, witness, cost, and causality from the script itself.

Self-review comes first. Independent review may help at the task's scale, but a fixed multi-agent team or a fixed number of review rounds is not required. Internal batch boundaries are not reasons to wait for the user. Resolve dependencies when an earlier batch has a substantive source conflict, preserve a recoverable version before editing, repair within scope, and deliver once necessary checks pass without material unresolved issues.

## Conflict and cathartic payoff

A useful design shorthand is **anticipation under pressure × contrast in status or capability × visible release**. This is a creative heuristic, not a mathematical measure. A display of superiority has less meaning when the story has not made its stakes understandable.

Three patterns in the source's action and male-audience examples:

1. **Identity or status dominance:** physical evidence exposes a concealed position in public—arriving aircraft or an exclusive financial credential, for example.
2. **Revenge or rebuttal:** at the antagonist's confident peak, the protagonist defeats the challenge decisively, such as stopping a heavy punch with one finger in a fantasy setting.
3. **Visual wonder:** an apparently powerless person's blood transforms scrap metal into a radiant artifact, under rules established by the story.

A genre-specific antagonist ladder: minor opponents may last 1–5 episodes, using contempt or public insults; intermediate opponents may last 5–25, exploiting money or rules; the primary antagonist may embody the series-long source of harm. Their defeats escalate from a small status reversal to exposure and loss of power to the final confrontation. The original action template uses violent defeat and spectacular destruction; use those only when the project's tone, authorization, and established world support them. The episode counts and violent outcomes are not universal requirements.

## Pacing and the three-act episode

Pacing manages changing audience emotion: what might the viewer feel now, and what will the next action change? Maintaining interest is the aim, not a guarantee of retention.

### A 2–3-minute episode illustration

| Portion | Dramatic function | Intended emotional movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening, approximately 30 seconds | Answer the previous hook and establish this episode's conflict promptly; the source's five-second cue is illustrative | Curiosity, tension, anger |
| Middle, approximately 1–1.5 minutes | Character collision, new information, escalating conflict, and a meaningful reversal where the story supports it | Anxiety, anticipation, empathy, rising anger |
| End, approximately 30–45 seconds | An episode payoff or emotional peak, then a specific continuation question; the source uses the last ten seconds as a cue | Release or surprise leading to renewed interest |

These ranges are sketches and may overlap; they are not timing measurements or universal episode lengths.

### Whole-series waves

For a roughly 60-episode cathartic serial, the source uses rising peaks with brief relief, ending in a larger climax and resolution. Scale to the contracted length and genre. Sweet romance may ignite quickly, sustain relationship tension, then resolve briskly; slow-burn mystery may accumulate evidence before a rapid cascade.

| Phase | Illustrative share | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening ignition | About 20% | Establish characters, central conflict, and opening cutoff |
| Development | About 30% | Expand conflict and introduce consequential people or forces |
| Converging climaxes | About 33% | Intersect storylines, reveal truth, enable the major reversal |
| Resolution | About 17% | Final confrontation, emotional completion, and ending |

| Position | Possible node |
| --- | --- |
| Episode 1 | A compelling opening and a clear predicament |
| Around 5% | First small victory, glimpse of identity, or new advantage |
| Chosen first paywall, historically around Episode 10 | First major break, divorce, reveal, or equivalent cutoff |
| Around 25% | A fundamental change in circumstances |
| Around 35% | A new force or more complex opponent; rotate meaningful antagonists rather than only inflating one villain |
| Around 50% | A midpoint reversal |
| Around two thirds | The protagonist's greatest crisis |
| Around 75% | The final counterattack begins |
| Final stretch | Converging climaxes, emotional resolution, and the chosen closed or open ending |

For long runs, connected secondary characters, several planted promises, and changing opponents can provide endurance. Do not mechanically add subplots to meet a count.

### Speed and breathing room

Accelerate with purposeful jump cuts, sharp dialogue exchanges, several related information turns in one scene, concise sentences, and genuine deadlines. Slow briefly for calm before a crisis, emotional preparation, or the consequences of a major revelation. The source suggests one or two minutes to absorb a revelation and a limit of roughly half an episode for slow passages; reconcile these illustrative cues with the actual runtime. Stillness should change understanding or relationships, not merely fill time.

| Reversal scale | Historical planning frequency | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Micro | 2–3 per episode | Small surprises or adjustments |
| Medium | Once every 3–5 episodes | A new direction or consequential information |
| Major | Once every 10–15 episodes | A changed understanding or power structure |
| Defining | 1–2 across the series | Core truth that transforms the story |

Use the frequencies as diagnostic prompts, never forced insertions or market facts.

### Episode-to-episode connection

The last image or line should create a coherent next step.

| Method | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Continuing speech | “You think you've won?” → the speaker turns: “There's one thing you missed.” |
| Continuing action | A hand swings to slap → another hand catches it |
| Answering the question | A door opens → the unexpected visitor is revealed |
| Deliberate scene switch | Scene A's unresolved question → a related conflict in Scene B, with A answered at a purposeful later point |

Do not replay the entire previous ending, dismiss the promised consequence, or open with a long recap. A delayed answer must earn its delay and preserve trust. Keep adjacent episodes from repeating the same location, confrontation, and hook shape without a changed function.
